\begin{abstract}
We present a handheld pendulum device as an exploratory interface that allows participants to feel and manipulate musical timing. The participant swings the pendulum, and a robotic glockenspiel instrument plays music with a tempo matching the pendulum at one beat per swing. The physical properties of the pendulum make an organic mapping that naturally gravitates towards a steady, plausible rhythm, while still intuitively open to explicit control. While particularly suitable as an introductory, educational means of musical engagement, this interface caters to varied levels of expertise, providing opportunities for expression by manifesting the participant's own understanding of the musical phrases. Social play is also encouraged through a multi-player cooperative mode. We envision that the approach of physically-guided, tactile, accessible interaction schemes can be extended to wider contexts and different media formats to foster participant exploration, discovery, and creativity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Throughout our experience, we have always been able to capture the instinctive desire to actively and earnestly engage in music from those that have not had the advantage of dedicated training. We believe that music, as a natural and fundamental means of human expression, has always been sparking interest in a wider audience who has the potential to establish a closer connection with it.

In our lasting endeavour to expand the reach of musically-meaningful interactive experiences, we set out on a search of an interaction scheme that can be naturally understood and operated without prior training, while navigating the participant in sensible directions to manipulate the sounds to their own imagination, opening spaces for individual expression.

The periodic motion of the pendulum caught our attention. The stability and self-recovering property of pendular motion makes for a reliable framework of periodic musical time, i.e., tempo, or beat duration, that plays a significant role across musical cultures.

In this demonstration, we present a controller interface, which we title \textit{Malletwand} (published in our previous research article~\cite{nime2024_59}), in the form of a handheld pendulum device (the ``wand''). By swinging the pendulum, the participant is able to feel and manipulate the speed of musical time, while the kinematic properties gently guide them through the tendency to keep a stable period, preventing excessive deviance from a steady tempo and thus a generally plausible sound. This makes it particularly suitable as an introductory, educational means of musical engagement. Meanwhile, the participant is also free to deliberately alter the timing by swinging at the speed they intend, so that they can control the speed and the phrasing of the music.

We imagine that this interface be paired with various types of musical actuators. For this demonstration, we build a self-playing glockenspiel (the ``mallet'') operated by up to two such controllers in order to demonstrate how our design encourages musical exploration and social play.

\section{Related Work}

Regular intervals of time, as the feature underpinning our interaction scheme, is a universal nature of human perception. There is abundant argument that humans instinctively tend to perceive time in cycles and patterns~\cite{Brower:Cog, Neisser_1976, Ravignani2016}. In particular, repeating cycles of time form grounds on which we anticipate our sensory stimuli, which explains why most music cultures have a sense of equal-spaced ``beats''. Discussions went further to establish the metaphorical and cognitive interplay of our sense of time with embodied motion~\cite{Johnson_Larson_2003, Johnson_2008}.

Indeed, there have been a number of devices that manifest the link between periodic bodily movement and stable temporal patterns. Scrubbing is a standard technique for turntablists to manipulate the speed of sounds by rotating vinyl records. Hand-cranked musical boxes provide a more approachable way to drive the flow of music by one's own hands; although the Swedish band Wintergatan went as far as to build an entire machine, the Marble Machine, that turns the revolution of the manual crank into the progression of a musical piece~\cite{Rundle_Woollaston-Webber_2017}.

These devices share the limitation that they require practice, or at least familarity with steady bodily movement, to produce legible and aesthetically pleasing sounds. Participants without prior training may invoke unsteady motion, rendering the music less understandable and plausible, without a clear hint on how the actions could be refined. Thus, while these work well as musical instruments, they do not make the most ideal introductory interactions on their own.

From another perspective, novel musical devices are already exploring the properties of pendular motion and handheld physical controllers. Kugelschwung~\cite{Kugelschwung} is a digital musical instrument that relies on a set of pendulums mounted on a tabletop frame supplying control signals for soundscapes. Le Bâton~\cite{LeBaton} maps the chaotic behaviour of the triple pendulum into unpredictably varying electronic sounds. Gyrotyre~\cite{Gyrotyre}, on the other hand (no pun intended), is a handheld controller that maps oscillating signals from a gyroscope installed on a precessing wheel to various musical applications.

Still, these interfaces and instruments take larger physical forms and mainly aim at stage performances and/or dedicated experimental production setups. Our design adds to this ensemble a missing piece: a lightweight handheld controller interface that can be enjoyable and meaningful regardless of the participant's prior musical experience.

\section{The ``Wand''}

\subsection{Design Overview}
The handheld controller interface takes the form of a handle with a hanging bob (ball) that can freely swing and spin around, forming a pendulum. The participant swings the pendulum at their desired frequency while the bob's quasi-periodic motion is mapped to continuous musical time such that each fastest-moving point in a period corresponds to the start of a beat. (This point is usually perceived to be the ``central'' or the ``lowest'' point in simple pendulums, but since ours has an extra degree of freedom, i.e., is a spherical pendulum, this appointment needs to be extrapolated.) Such generated timing signals are wirelessly transmitted to an actuator, such as a self-playing instrument, that plays a piece of music according to the manipulated flow of musical time.

We add a few simple features to enrich the feedback and the interaction. Haptic feedback at beat onsets is provided by a vibration motor. A colourful LED is placed inside the bob that is flexible in its responses to interaction, e.g., it may indicate the changing beats by alternating tints or identify the voice by colour coding in the case of multiplayer interaction. Furthermore, the controller is intended to be normally at rest, so it is equipped with a tilt sensor that wakes up the entire circuit from sleep when the device is picked up by a participant. A single-pole, single-throw (SPST) switch allows the overall power to be turned off during times without participants.

\subsection{Construction and Implementation}
We design customised electronics for all of our devices. In addition to the aforementioned components, the handle contains a lithium polymer (Li-Po) battery, a microcontroller, and a radio transceiver; the bob contains a separate low-power microcontroller and MEMS chips that form a 9-axis IMU (an accelerometer, a gyroscope, and a geomagnetometer). The complete structure is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:WandConstruction}.

\begin{figure*}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{nime/Mw_Wand.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Photo of the assembled circuit board before being cut. (b) Photo of the assembled wand with the casing and the bob. (c) Block diagram of the electronic components involved.}
  \label{fig:WandConstruction}
\end{figure*}

The board is fabricated as a single unit, in order to ease soldering and debugging as we build the prototype. After testing is complete, we cut the board at the thin connection (in Figure~\ref{fig:WandConstruction}a) and connect the two parts with soldered thin wires (3 strands of 32 AWG, establishing a power supply and an asynchronous USART connection). The handle part is glued inside a 3D-printed casing with the wires hanging down onto the bob part (Figure~\ref{fig:WandConstruction}b), which is inserted onto a holder made of polymeric foam glued in a translucent plastic sphere filled with down cotton. We add fishing wires to strengthen the connection and prevent the electric wires from excessive tension and fatigue.

A dedicated algorithm is designed to derive a smooth timing signal from the sensor readings, so that the pendulum's phase of motion is estimated within its elliptic trajectory, which is then mapped to a steady, uniform lapse of time. In this way, subdivisions of a beat also closely follow the pendulum's motion (and changes thereof), improving the smoothness and the responsiveness of control. The main processing steps comprise a combination of the complementary filter and the extended Kalman filter (EKF). Our research article~\cite{nime2024_59} elaborates on the details of this algorithm.

\section{The ``Mallets''}

\begin{figure*}[th!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{nime/Mw_Mallet.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Overall photo of the instrument. (b) Close-up of a single sub-unit. (c) Block diagram of the electronic components involved.}
  \label{fig:MalletConstruction}
\end{figure*}

Our self-playing instrument is a robotic glockenspiel. We use a 3-key prototype in the photos, but an extension of register (a 21-key installation) is identical in implementation and will be presented onsite.

The keys are laser-cut from aluminium of 5 mm thickness, calibrated to the fabricator's sample material and programatically designed in the OpenSCAD software. The electronics of the instrument consists of a main unit, which handles power and communication, and multiple sub-units, one for each key, comprising a brushless motor, a colourful LED, and supporting circuitry. We also build our own little yarn mallets. Figure~\ref{fig:MalletConstruction} outlines the components involved, with finer details of the construction explained in our research article~\cite{nime2024_59}.

The instrument is accompanied by two Malletwand controllers and can operate in both solo and co-operative mode. Signals received from the controllers drive the musical time in a preprogrammed piece, similar to a musical box. Co-operative mode is entered by two controllers being operated simultaneously, where each controller corresponds to one voice (part) indicated by colours of the lights. The music only progresses when the motion of the two are roughly in synchronisation.

During operation, the glockenspiel plays a tune as the pendulums swing back and forth. The tempo of the music matches the period of pendular motion, at one beat per swing. Haptic feedback (one vibration per beat) and lights on both the bob and the keys add to the embodied musical experience. Figure~\ref{fig:Banner} is a photo of the instrument in action. Please refer to the demonstration video for a performance recording.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Experience}
Our pilot study with outside participants brought forth a few insights. We confirmed that the pendulum-based interface is appreciated for its accuracy and ease of use. First-time users might not instantly figure out how to reliably manipulate the speed (period) of the pendulum, but were able to do so with their own exploration or with a hint. Since participants were able to quickly grasp the mapping logic and its control, musical exploration was instantly opened up to them. Participants tended to experiment with extremes before resorting to a steady tempo, trying gentler articulations. They considered the interaction intuitive and manageable, and reported being generally successful in their pursuit of musical engagement and expression.

Social play is encouraged through the attempts to synchronise between the duo. This resembles the experience of musicians in ensembles, orchestras, or bands, and is appreciated by such practitioners. However, we notice that this is a rare and precious chance for participants with less musical experiences, and we hope to bring such meaningful and joyful activities to their reach through our design. We are still yet to conduct more extensive studies with such participants.

One exposed characteristic of the co-operative mode is the friction in the duo's phase synchronisation, due to inertia preventing quick changes in speed, even more so regarding phase and position. This has the benefit of extending conversations and explorations between participants, but the less experienced of them might not figure out effective ways quickly and intuitively. In this regard, we are considering improving on the co-operative play mode by extending the haptic and visual feedback to provide hints for adjustment (faster/slower).

\subsection{Extrapolations and Future Work}
As we have written in the Introduction section, we imagine that this interface not be limited to our glockenspiel instrument, nor is the form of a pendulum imposed. Any device that plays music can employ a cyclic physical interface to invite participants into musical time. Hybrid formats, including projections and mappings, or musical devices remotely incited beyond kilometres.

Other musical features or temporal formats, or even spatial expositions, may be involved as well. The estimation algorithms in the pendulum enable it to act as a standalone pose sensor for dangling objects, adding to the toolkit of digital artistic expression.

Another possible extrapolation lies in the mobile scenario. The sensors we used are standard in today's smartphones, and relevant APIs are readily available on modern web browsers. It is thus possible for websites to enable similar interactive experiences without special equipment, simply by asking the visitor to put their phone in a holder, attach a string, and swing it to their liking. We implement a web-based preliminary demonstration of such a musical box, so that remote participants can also have a conceptually identical experience, while also providing a foundation for researchers and creators interested in potential extensions.

\section{Conclusion}
Malletwand is our first step in spreading the enjoyability of music through a tactile medium. It is to our delight to implement and demonstrate the system and its unique characteristics, adding to the state-of-the-art repertoire of physical-system controller interfaces. We realise that this interface has the potential to adapt to more instruments and scenarios, and put forward what we have envisaged. We share our work in the hope that our methods and insights can be useful to those who share the aspirations with us.
